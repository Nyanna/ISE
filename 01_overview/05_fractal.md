## **1.5. Fractal Nature**

The theory can be understood in relation to its fractal nature, drawing parallels with established fractal theories and existing cosmological observations. By comparing ISE with known fractal structures in both cosmology and quantum physics, we can explore its potential implications for our understanding of the universe on multiple scales.

Fractals are self-similar patterns that repeat at different scales, meaning that a zoom into any portion of the structure reveals a pattern that resembles the larger whole. This quality aligns with a picture in which the universe evolves through **continuous differentiation** across scales. Differentiation creates new structures, much like how a fractal branches out indefinitely but retains a pattern recognizable across levels of observation.

The universe is seen as **infinitely expanding**, but not in the classical sense of space expanding. Instead, new "layers" of reality differentiate from existing ones. This continuous process of differentiation parallels the concept of fractals: the universe on a smaller scale mirrors the universe on larger scales, with every level of differentiation creating new states of energy and structure. This aligns with the **recursive nature** of fractals, where structures repeat, becoming more complex while following a fundamental set of rules.

However, it is essential to state precisely what "fractal" means here — and what it does not. ISE does **not** claim literal geometric self-similarity between concrete objects across scales. Electrons are not planets; atomic orbitals are not miniature solar systems; a galaxy is not a large atom. What recurs across scales is the **organizing logic** — differentiation, resonance, constraint — not the morphology. The frequency-domain formalism developed in later chapters makes this distinction concrete and intuitable.

**The Fractal as Sampling Hierarchy**

The working picture here is a single continuous substrate — protoinformation as white noise with infinite frequency content. Physical structure at any scale is what this substrate looks like when sampled at a particular rate. A sampling rate $R$ resolves frequencies up to $R/2$ (the Nyquist limit) and nothing finer. Everything above that limit is not absent — it is unresolvable at that rate.

This single principle generates the entire fractal hierarchy:

* **Supersampling** means applying a higher sampling rate. More spectral content becomes visible, finer structure appears. Zooming in on a proton at electromagnetic sampling rates shows a featureless charge; supersampling at strong-interaction rates reveals quarks and gluons — not because they were hidden, but because they exist at frequencies the coarser rate cannot resolve.

* **Subsampling** means applying a lower sampling rate. Crucially, subsampling is **not averaging**. It is **aliasing**. When complex frequency structure is sampled below its Nyquist rate, unresolved frequencies **fold back** into the resolved band as aliased contributions. The result is a **moiré effect**: new apparent patterns — standing or running frequencies — that do not exist in the original structure but emerge from the interaction between spectral content and sampling rate. Whether these appear as standing or running frequencies depends on the projection — on which components fold onto which resolved bands and with what phase relationships.

This distinction matters: averaging would produce homogeneity — a featureless mean. Aliasing produces **structure**: interference patterns, apparent periodicities, large-scale correlations that are properties of the sampling relationship, not of the underlying spectrum.

The fractal arises because this process is **recursive and unbounded**: every sampling level can itself be supersampled to reveal finer structure, and subsampled to produce new aliased patterns at coarser scales, without limit in either direction. The hierarchy of sampling levels IS the fractal — not as a geometric pattern repeating in space, but as spectral structure generating new interference patterns at every rate.

Classical fractals (Mandelbrot sets, Koch snowflakes) are discrete iterations of a fixed transformation. The sampling-hierarchy picture considered here is **continuous** instead: between any two sampling rates exist infinitely many intermediate rates, each revealing slightly different spectral content and generating different aliasing patterns. The organizing logic recurs; the specific forms do not.

**Comparison with Fractal Cosmology**

The **Fractal Universe Hypothesis**, proposed in the 1980s, suggested that the universe might exhibit fractal patterns in the distribution of galaxies. Observations of galaxy clusters, superclusters, and filaments revealed patterns that appeared somewhat fractal. Galaxies and their large-scale structures are not uniformly distributed but are clustered in such a way that, at certain scales, these clusters resemble smaller versions of themselves.

The present account does not focus specifically on galaxy distribution, but it adopts a **similar fractal-like structure** at the level of energy differentiation. Each scale of differentiation can be read as a layer in the fractal. On smaller scales, energy differentiates into matter and forces (e.g., atomic particles); on larger scales, into galaxies, voids, and cosmic structures. This is consistent with the idea of **fractal cosmology**, where structures repeat across scales. The classical fractal universe hypothesis tapers at around 100 million light-years, reflecting the observed homogeneity on large scales. The sampling-hierarchy reading proposed here extends the differentiation picture indefinitely in both directions — as an interpretive extrapolation, the fractal logic is assumed to continue both below the Planck scale and beyond the observable universe.

**Fractals in Quantum Physics**

In **quantum physics**, particularly in **loop quantum gravity** and some interpretations of **string theory**, there are hints of fractal-like behaviors at extremely small scales. For instance, space-time itself might have **fractal properties** at the Planck scale (about $10^{-35}$ meters), where classical concepts of space and time break down. In this context, space-time could be seen as a fractal object, with its structure becoming more complex and irregular the closer one examines it.

The picture developed here is compatible with this line of thought: **space and time are treated as emergent properties** of energy differentiation rather than fundamental entities. Just as fractals reveal more complexity on zooming in, at smaller scales more energy states differentiate, giving rise to new forms of matter and interactions. This continuous differentiation mirrors how quantum mechanics allows for superposition and fluctuation at tiny scales, which only stabilize into classical forms when observed on larger scales. In the frequency-domain reading, "zooming in" is supersampling: applying a higher rate to the same substrate, resolving spectral content that was above the Nyquist limit at coarser rates. What appears as "quantum weirdness" from a macroscopic sampling perspective is ordinary resolved structure at quantum rates — an analogy, not a derivation.

**Points of Contact with Other Fractal Accounts**

Both the sampling-hierarchy reading used here and traditional fractal theories of the universe suggest self-similarity across scales. A few points of emphasis differ:

* **Open-ended differentiation** (interpretive): Fractal structures in nature (snowflakes, coastlines) usually have a limit to their recursive patterns due to physical constraints. The working assumption taken here is that differentiation remains open-ended across the scales considered, with new layers unfolding at each — a modelling choice rather than an observational claim.  
* **Emergence of space and time** (interpretive): Reality is treated as emergent from energy differentiation, so that what we perceive as matter, time, and space can be read as byproducts of this process. Many fractal models instead describe objects and distributions within a pre-defined space and time; the present reading folds space-time itself into the differentiation picture.  
* **Subsampling as aliasing, not smoothing** (derivation): Coarser sampling of a spectrum-rich substrate does not produce a simple average. Standard signal-processing results show that it produces **aliasing** — moiré-like interference patterns that generate new apparent structure at larger scales. This offers one account of why observed structure persists at every scale rather than converging toward featurelessness.

**Homogeneity as Projection**

The **Fractal Universe Hypothesis** was challenged by evidence that, on very large scales (over 100 million light-years), the universe appears homogeneous and isotropic, not fractal. ISE addresses this differently than simply proposing that differentiation continues at sub-galactic scales.

No two radians of our observational horizon are the same. The CMB sky is a detailed map of anisotropies at the $10^{-5}$ level, with every patch carrying a unique spectral signature. Galaxy surveys reveal unique structure in every direction: no two filaments are identical, no two voids have the same shape. The universe is, at every observable angular resolution, **everywhere different**.

It becomes "homogeneous" only through **projection onto a specific basis** — typically energy density or matter density averaged over a chosen volume. This projection discards all structural information that does not contribute to the chosen scalar quantity. The result is smooth not because the universe is smooth but because the projection basis is too coarse to carry the structure.

This is not an artifact of limited instrumental resolution. Even with perfect instruments, the choice to represent the result as "average density per $100$ Mpc$^3$ volume" would produce homogeneity — because that representation discards precisely the information distinguishing one region from another. In the frequency-domain picture, projecting a rich spectral structure onto a single scalar (energy density) is equivalent to retaining only the zeroth moment of the frequency profile. Of course the result appears uniform — the zeroth moment of any sufficiently extended spectrum is approximately constant. But the spectrum itself is not constant.

The question "is the universe homogeneous?" is therefore incomplete without specifying: **homogeneous with respect to which projection basis?** Projected onto energy density — yes, approximately, above certain scales. Projected onto spectral structure — no, everywhere different, at every scale. The cosmological principle is not wrong; it is a statement about one particular projection, elevated by convention to an ontological claim.

**The Definitional Incoherence of Physical Homogeneity**

The argument extends beyond projection dependence. Homogeneity is not merely basis-relative — it is internally contradictory as a physical state. The concept requires "everywhere the same, but at different locations." The qualification "at different locations" presupposes **spatial distinguishability**: the ability to tell one point from another. Distinguishability is differentiation — it requires that at least one physical quantity varies between positions. But "everywhere the same" demands the absence of precisely this variation. The concept contains its own negation. A physical state that is simultaneously everywhere identical and everywhere distinguishable cannot exist.

The standard qualifier "approximately homogeneous" does not rescue the concept; it constitutes its entire content. Fully unpacked, "the universe is approximately homogeneous on large scales" means: freeze time, ignore all scales below a chosen threshold, reduce the state space to a single scalar (density), treat gravitation as background geometry rather than participant, and observe that the remainder looks roughly uniform. Each operation actively removes differentiation. What remains is not a discovered property of the universe but a constructed property of the chain of omissions. The qualifier "approximately" marks not a minor empirical concession but the entire distance between the claim and reality.

This has a direct structural consequence. The cosmological principle cannot claim **superscalar validity** — validity across all scales — because it exists only through scale-truncation. Homogeneity arises by discarding structure below a threshold; it therefore cannot hold across scales that include that threshold. "Superscalar homogeneity" is self-refuting: the concept presupposes the scale-limitation whose absence it claims.

The empirical record is exceptionless on this point. Every improvement in observational resolution has revealed more structure, not less. The CMB appeared featureless to early measurements; COBE resolved anisotropies at $10^{-5}$; WMAP refined these into acoustic peaks with detailed spectral structure; Planck revealed polarisation fine structure and lensing signatures within the anisotropies. Galaxy surveys at every generation — from CfA to SDSS to DESI — have uncovered filamentary distributions at every measured scale, with no convergence toward uniformity. The pattern admits a concise statement: higher resolution reveals more differentiation, without exception. This is consistent with incommensurability as the generic state and commensurability as the special case requiring specific symmetry conditions.

The zeroth-moment argument explains why the projection appears uniform: retaining only the zeroth moment of a rich frequency profile discards exactly the spectral content that constitutes differentiation. The definitional refutation explains why it must be so — homogeneity as a physical state would require distinguishable locations without distinguishing properties, which is a contradiction in terms. The physical argument (*Gravitational Irreducibility*) and the thermodynamic consequence (*Space–Entropy Coupling*) develop this further: interaction-driven self-coupling prevents convergence to homogeneity dynamically, and maximal entropy under self-coupling is structured, not uniform. The complete classical derivation chain assembling these results into a formal theorem appears in *Classical Closure*. Homogeneity is commensurability; commensurability is the special case, not the generic condition.

**A Recursive, Fractal-like Model**

The universe is not just infinite in space or time but **infinitely recursive**, with reality continually differentiating across scales. This idea mirrors **fractal cosmology** and the self-similarity seen in fractal patterns, but with a more profound implication: the universe is **dynamic**, constantly evolving, and never reaches a "final" state.

Classical fractal theories describe objects and distributions within a fixed space-time. The reading developed here treats **space and time as part of the fractal-like unfolding** itself — an interpretive move rather than a proved result. Physical scales can be arranged illustratively:

$$\text{Vacuum} \leftrightarrow \text{Particles} \leftrightarrow \text{Atoms} \leftrightarrow \text{Molecules} \leftrightarrow \text{Stars} \leftrightarrow \text{Galaxies} \leftrightarrow \text{Cosmic Web} \leftrightarrow \text{?}$$

The arrows are bidirectional — supersampling moves left, subsampling moves right. At each position, the same structural situation holds: a finite sampling rate produces resolved content plus aliased contributions from unresolved content. Moving rightward (coarser rates), aliased contributions generate moiré-like patterns at larger scales — real in the projection but not present in the substrate at those frequencies. Moving leftward (finer rates), previously aliased contributions become resolvable, and new aliasing patterns emerge from still-deeper spectral content. The recursion has no terminus in either direction.

It is tempting to present specific subsampling chains as definitive mechanisms — electromagnetic physics subsampled into hydrodynamics, hydrodynamics into gravitational structure. Such chains serve as illustrative examples, but presenting any particular chain as THE mechanism would be a category error. The point is not which spectral content gets subsampled through which sequence of rates. The point is that **at every scale, the same structural situation obtains**: a finite sampling rate applied to spectral content exceeding its Nyquist limit, producing resolved structure, aliased contributions, and moiré-like emergence of patterns belonging to neither substrate nor sampling rate alone but to their interaction.

In summary, the picture can be read as **a fractal process**: structure across the scales considered is treated as an evolving, scale-free system that mirrors the recursive patterns familiar from natural fractals, extended here across both quantum and cosmic ranges. The fractal is not taken to sit in the shapes. It sits in the sampling — and in the moiré structure that sampling generates from a spectrum richer than any finite rate can resolve.
