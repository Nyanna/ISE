# Superconductivity as Macroscopic Resonance Closure

## The Standard Account

The dominant theoretical framework for superconductivity is the BCS theory (Bardeen, Cooper, Schrieffer, 1957). In this account, an electron traversing a metallic lattice locally deforms the ionic structure through phonon emission. The resulting transient accumulation of positive charge density attracts a second electron. This phonon-mediated attraction overcomes the Coulomb repulsion between the two electrons, binding them into a Cooper pair — a composite boson with total spin zero and opposite momenta. Because Cooper pairs are bosons, they are exempt from the Pauli exclusion principle and may collectively occupy the same quantum ground state, forming a macroscopic Bose–Einstein condensate in momentum space. This condensate is described by a single wavefunction with a globally defined phase. Resistance vanishes because individual scattering events cannot eject a Cooper pair from the condensate: doing so would require overcoming the BCS energy gap $\Delta$, which thermal fluctuations below the critical temperature $T_c$ cannot provide. As long as no scattering process delivers sufficient energy to break a pair, no dissipative channel exists.

The BCS wavefunction is not a collection of identifiable pairs but a collective state over the entire Fermi sea:

$$|\Psi_{\text{BCS}}\rangle = \prod_{\mathbf{k}} \left( u_{\mathbf{k}} + v_{\mathbf{k}} \, c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow} c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow} \right) |0\rangle$$

For each momentum $\mathbf{k}$, the amplitudes $u_{\mathbf{k}}$ and $v_{\mathbf{k}}$ describe the probability of the state $(\mathbf{k}\!\uparrow, -\mathbf{k}\!\downarrow)$ being occupied or empty. The product runs over all $\mathbf{k}$ simultaneously. No individual electron is assigned to a specific partner; the pairing is a collective correlation structure across the entire momentum space. The coherence length $\xi$ — which can reach thousands of ångströms in conventional superconductors — measures the spatial range of this correlation, within which millions of such correlations overlap.

For conventional superconductors — elemental metals, intermetallic compounds, and the recently discovered hydrogen-rich superconductors under extreme pressure — BCS theory and its extensions (Eliashberg theory, Migdal approximation) provide quantitatively successful descriptions. The phonon-mediated coupling mechanism is well-established and experimentally confirmed.

For unconventional superconductors — cuprates, iron-based pnictides and chalcogenides, heavy-fermion systems, and nickelate superconductors — the coupling mechanism remains an open problem. Phonon exchange alone cannot account for critical temperatures exceeding 100 K. The most widely discussed alternative proposals involve antiferromagnetic spin fluctuations, charge fluctuations, or more exotic mechanisms such as pair density waves. Despite decades of intense research, no consensus has emerged, and the origin of high-temperature superconductivity remains one of the central unsolved problems in condensed matter physics.

## Spectral Reformulation

Within the ISE framework, superconductivity is the establishment of macroscopic phase coherence in the electromagnetic frequency band. The phenomenon requires no new ontological category; it is a direct consequence of the same resonance closure conditions that govern particle stability, confinement, and binding throughout the theory. The framework identifies two distinct pathways to closure — *dynamic stabilization* and *dimensional reduction* — which correspond to qualitatively different physical regimes but share the same underlying mechanism.

**Frequency Profiles and Spectral Deficits.** Every conduction electron is a frequency profile — an amplitude-phase distribution across hierarchically sampled differentiation bands. In a normal metal, these profiles possess spectral deficits: frequency bands in which the amplitude-phase structure is not self-consistent. These open bands are the spectral signature of what conventional theory describes as available states near the Fermi surface.

**Complementary Deficits and Pair Formation.** When two frequency profiles possess complementary spectral deficits — open bands whose amplitude-phase structures are mutually completing — their superposition generates a composite pattern at a higher differentiation depth. The previously open bands are superseded by the emergent spectral structure of the superposition. This is the ISE description of Cooper pair formation: two profiles whose deficits are complementary (in BCS language: time-reversed states $(\mathbf{k}\!\uparrow, -\mathbf{k}\!\downarrow)$) combine into a closed resonance structure that neither profile contained individually.

**Resonance Closure as Self-Consistency.** The composite profile satisfies a closure condition: its internal phase relations are self-resonant and require no external stabilization. This is formally identical to the non-trivial solution of the BCS gap equation,

$$\Delta_{\mathbf{k}} = -\sum_{\mathbf{k}'} V_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}'} \frac{\Delta_{\mathbf{k}'}}{2E_{\mathbf{k}'}},$$

which admits either the trivial solution $\Delta = 0$ (normal state: no resonance closure) or a non-trivial solution $\Delta \neq 0$ (superconducting state: closure achieved). The ISE term *resonance closure* designates precisely this bifurcation: the self-consistency condition is satisfied, and the frequency profile space admits a stable, closed configuration.

**The Energy Gap as Closure Stability.** The BCS gap $\Delta$ corresponds to the minimum energy required to disrupt the resonance closure locally — to re-open spectral bands that the composite profile had closed. Below $T_c$, the thermal broadband frequency structure of the lattice lacks sufficient spectral overlap with the closed profile to induce phase drift. Above $T_c$, the thermal spectrum overlaps sufficiently with the paired profile's frequency content to destroy the phase coherence that sustains closure.

**Macroscopic Phase Coherence.** In the superconducting state, the resonance closure extends across the entire sample. The macroscopic order parameter $\Psi = |\Psi|e^{i\varphi}$ with a globally defined phase $\varphi$ is the direct expression of this: the closed profile structure is not local but system-wide. This is the analogue in the electromagnetic frequency band of the color-neutral closure condition that defines hadrons in the strong-interaction band — but realized at macroscopic coherence length rather than at femtometer scales.

## Two Pathways to Closure

The resonance closure condition can be satisfied through two fundamentally different mechanisms. Their distinction is central to understanding why superconductivity appears across such diverse material classes with such different critical temperatures.

**Pathway I: Dynamic Stabilization.** In conventional superconductors at ambient or moderate pressures, the electronic phase space is high-dimensional. Electrons possess many degrees of freedom into which they can scatter, and the Fermi surface is a complex manifold in momentum space. Resonance closure in this regime is a *dynamic* achievement: the lattice (via phonons, spin fluctuations, or other mediators) provides an effective attraction that correlates complementary deficit profiles across the Fermi surface. The closure is maintained against thermal disruption through continuous dynamic stabilization — a delicate balance between the attractive correlation and the entropic tendency toward decoherence.

In this regime, the coherence length $\xi$ is a meaningful and central parameter. It measures the spatial range over which the dynamic correlation can be sustained. Large $\xi$ (thousands of ångströms in elemental superconductors) reflects weak coupling: the correlation is fragile and extends over many lattice constants precisely because it must recruit a large volume of phase space to achieve closure. The BCS gap $\Delta$ is correspondingly small, and $T_c$ is low.

**Pathway II: Dimensional Reduction.** Under extreme compression, the available electronic phase space collapses. The lattice geometry constrains the electronic degrees of freedom so severely that the frequency profiles of the conduction electrons are forced into a low-dimensional subspace. In the limiting case, the profiles are confined to a single axis with only two possible phase orientations — complementary by construction.

In this regime, resonance closure is not dynamically stabilized but *geometrically enforced*. Scattering is eliminated not because an energy gap protects the paired state, but because the phase space contains no states into which scattering could occur. The closure is trivial in the mathematical sense: the self-consistency condition is automatically satisfied when the space of solutions contains only complementary configurations.

The coherence length $\xi$ loses its physical significance in this regime. The concept presupposes that decoherence is possible and that the correlation has a finite range — but when the phase space itself admits only closed configurations, coherence is not a property that can degrade with distance. It is a structural identity of the available state space. Asking for the coherence length is like asking how far a one-lane road can maintain single-file traffic: the question presupposes alternatives that do not exist.

This distinction explains a pattern that BCS theory can accommodate mathematically but not conceptually: the hydrogen-rich superhydride superconductors (H$_3$S at 203 K, LaH$_{10}$ at 250 K, YH$_9$ at 244 K) achieve critical temperatures that are anomalously high even within the Eliashberg framework. The standard explanation appeals to exceptionally strong electron-phonon coupling combined with high phonon frequencies — both consequences of the light hydrogen mass under megabar compression. ISE reframes this: the extreme pressure does not merely strengthen the coupling within a high-dimensional phase space; it *collapses the phase space itself*. The light hydrogen atoms under compression have both extremely high characteristic frequencies and extremely restricted geometric freedom. The result is not stronger dynamic stabilization but a crossover toward dimensional reduction — closure becomes increasingly geometric rather than dynamic, and $T_c$ rises not because the coupling constant increases but because the conditions under which closure fails become progressively harder to realize.

## High-Temperature Superconductors and Structural Predictions

The ISE framework generates several structural predictions that align with the empirical properties of high-temperature superconductors. The two-pathway structure developed above provides the interpretive key.

**Layered Structure as Partial Dimensional Reduction.** All known high-$T_c$ superconductor families at ambient pressure share a common structural feature: highly ordered, quasi-two-dimensional layers (CuO$_2$ planes in cuprates, FeAs/FeSe layers in iron-based superconductors). ISE predicts this directly: the layered geometry represents a partial dimensional reduction at ambient pressure. The electronic degrees of freedom are constrained to quasi-two-dimensional manifolds, reducing the phase space available for scattering and lowering the threshold for closure. Disordered or three-dimensionally isotropic structures lack this geometric constraint and therefore require either lower temperatures (more dynamic stabilization) or external pressure (more dimensional reduction) to achieve closure. High-$T_c$ materials at ambient pressure are layered because the crystal geometry performs a structural function that extreme pressure achieves by brute force.

**Anisotropy as Closure Directionality.** Cuprate superconductors are extremely anisotropic — their superconducting properties depend strongly on crystallographic direction, with coherence lengths of 1–10 Å perpendicular to the planes versus substantially longer values within the planes. ISE interprets this as the natural consequence of resonance closure establishing itself preferentially along the directions in which the dimensional reduction is most effective. Within the planes, the constrained phase space supports robust closure. Perpendicular to the planes, the full three-dimensional phase space is available, scattering channels proliferate, and coherence is correspondingly limited.

**Short Coherence Length at High $T_c$.** The coherence length $\xi$ in high-$T_c$ materials (1–10 Å) is orders of magnitude shorter than in conventional superconductors (thousands of Å). Within the two-pathway framework, this has a precise interpretation: high-$T_c$ materials at ambient pressure occupy an intermediate position between pure dynamic stabilization (Pathway I, large $\xi$, low $T_c$) and pure dimensional reduction (Pathway II, $\xi$ undefined, high $T_c$). The short coherence length reflects the fact that the partial dimensional reduction in layered structures tightens the coupling — the closure is sustained over shorter distances because the reduced phase space makes it more robust. In the limit of full dimensional reduction under extreme pressure, $\xi$ ceases to be a relevant parameter entirely.

**Pressure Enhancement of $T_c$ as Phase-Space Collapse.** Hydrostatic pressure systematically raises $T_c$ across many superconducting families. The most dramatic examples are the superhydrides: H$_3$S achieves $T_c = 203$ K at 150 GPa, LaH$_{10}$ reaches $T_c \approx 250$ K at comparable pressures. ISE reads this not as stronger coupling within an unchanged phase space, but as progressive dimensional reduction of the phase space itself. Compression restricts the geometric degrees of freedom available to the conduction electrons, driving the system from Pathway I toward Pathway II. The crossover is continuous: as pressure increases, the closure becomes increasingly geometric and decreasingly dynamic, $T_c$ rises, and the coherence length loses interpretive significance. The superhydrides represent the most advanced point on this continuum currently accessible — systems in which the light hydrogen atoms under megabar compression approach the limit of geometrically enforced closure.

**Magnetism as Reorganizable Coherence.** Undoped cuprates are antiferromagnetic insulators. Doping destroys the magnetic order and, within a specific doping range, enables superconductivity. The coexistence of magnetic fluctuations near $T_c$ has led to proposals that spin fluctuations provide the pairing glue in cuprates. ISE offers a unified reading: magnetic order is itself a form of phase coherence — in the spin-frequency band rather than the charge-frequency band. Doping disrupts this rigid spin coherence, liberating spectral degrees of freedom whose deficit structure becomes available for reorganization into electromagnetic-band closure. The magnetic fluctuations observed near $T_c$ are not the pairing mechanism; they are the spectral residue of the dissolving spin coherence whose structural deficits now participate in charge-band resonance closure.

This reinterpretation dissolves the long-standing debate over the pairing mechanism in unconventional superconductors. ISE does not require a choice between phonons, magnons, or electron–electron interactions as competing coupling mechanisms. These are different projective descriptions of the same underlying process: complementary spectral deficits closing into a coherent resonance structure. The dominant frequency band structure of the lattice determines which projective description appears most natural, but the mechanism — resonance closure — is always the same.

## Stochastic Resonance Closure and the Limits of Dimensional Reduction

The relationship between pressure and superconductivity is not monotonic. The two-pathway framework predicts a specific failure mode: dimensional reduction enhances closure only as long as the compression is globally uniform — only as long as all electronic profiles are forced into the same reduced subspace across the entire sample. When the lattice can no longer sustain this uniformity, the system enters a qualitatively distinct regime.

ISE predicts a sequence of three regimes as pressure increases:

**Regime I: Coherent Closure.** At moderate pressures, the lattice retains its ordered structure. Compression enhances closure — either by strengthening dynamic stabilization (moderate pressure on layered materials) or by progressing toward dimensional reduction (higher pressure, especially in hydrogen-rich systems). The superconducting transition is sharp: the gap equation has a clean non-trivial solution, the order parameter is spatially homogeneous, and resistance drops abruptly to zero.

**Regime II: Stochastic Percolative Closure.** At higher pressures, the lattice undergoes structural transitions — phase boundaries between crystal structures emerge, local symmetry breaking occurs, and the uniform geometry that supported global dimensional reduction fragments into domains. Within each domain, the local phase-space reduction may still enforce closure. But adjacent domains, separated by structural defects, phase boundaries, or symmetry mismatches, are not mutually coherent. The dimensional reduction succeeds locally but fails globally.

In this regime, the system consists of superconducting islands — local agglomerates in which resonance closure is geometrically enforced — embedded in a matrix of normal or weakly conducting material. Superconductivity in the macroscopic sense depends on percolation: a current can traverse the sample without dissipation if and only if a connected path of closed domains spans the sample. Because the closure domains fluctuate temporally — they form, persist, and dissolve as thermal and structural fluctuations modulate the local phase-space geometry — the percolative path is stochastic. At any given moment, a path is statistically likely but not guaranteed.

The observable consequences are:

- **Broadened transition**: $T_c$ is no longer a sharp bifurcation point but a gradual crossover. The resistance decreases continuously over a temperature range as the density and connectivity of closure domains increases with cooling.
- **Incomplete transition**: The resistance may not reach zero even at the lowest temperatures, because the percolative path is intermittent. Residual resistance persists — far below the normal-state value but measurably above zero.
- **Reduced critical current density**: The stochastic path can sustain less current than a homogeneous superconductor, because the effective cross-section of the connected superconducting path fluctuates and the weakest link determines the capacity.

**Regime III: Closure Collapse.** At still higher pressures, the structural deformation becomes so severe that even local dimensional reduction fails. No domain retains sufficient geometric order to enforce phase-space collapse. The electronic profiles return to a high-dimensional, disordered configuration in which neither dynamic stabilization (no coherent lattice to mediate coupling) nor dimensional reduction (no uniform compression axis) can produce closure. The material reverts to a normal or non-metallic state.

The critical structural variable governing the transitions between regimes is not pressure per se but *geometric uniformity of the dimensional reduction*. Pressure that preserves lattice order drives the system deeper into Regime I. Pressure that induces structural phase transitions, grain boundaries, or amorphization pushes the system into Regime II and eventually Regime III. This is why the regime boundaries are material-dependent and sensitive to pressure conditions (hydrostatic vs. non-hydrostatic), crystal quality, and proximity to structural phase transitions.

## Experimental Evidence for Stochastic Closure

The predicted sequence of regimes is directly observed in several high-pressure superconductor systems:

**Mercury-based cuprates (Hg1212, Hg1223).** In HgBa$_2$CaCu$_2$O$_{6+\delta}$, the superconducting transition broadens conspicuously under pressure in the overdoped regime. The same broadening appears in the underdoped regime at pressures above 15 GPa, precisely where $T_c$ begins to decrease. The authors of the systematic study describe the broadening as unexplained within their framework. ISE identifies it as the onset of Regime II: the lattice structure at these pressures can no longer sustain the geometric uniformity required for coherent closure across the sample.

**Iron-based superconductor FeSe$_{0.89}$S$_{0.11}$.** Under increasing pressure, this compound exhibits a sequence that maps precisely onto the three ISE regimes. Below 4 GPa, $T_c$ increases sharply (Regime I). Above 4 GPa, the transition broadens significantly; above 5 GPa, the resistance no longer reaches zero even at the lowest accessible temperatures, accompanied by an anomalously small critical current density (Regime II). The normal-state resistivity simultaneously develops non-metallic character. The authors describe this as "electronic phase separation" resulting from a structural transition — a descriptive label that ISE replaces with a mechanistic account: the structural transition has fragmented the globally uniform dimensional reduction into isolated domains that achieve closure only locally and stochastically.

**Elemental sulfur under extreme pressure.** Between 119 and 136 GPa, sulfur exhibits a broadened resistance drop during the gradual transition between crystal phases (body-centered orthorhombic S-IV to $\beta$-Po S-V). Above 155 GPa, the transition sharpens again as the sample fully enters the S-V phase. This sequence is a direct illustration of the regime structure: the phase coexistence region is Regime II (neither crystal structure spans the sample, closure is percolative across phase-mixed regions), while the homogeneous S-V phase restores Regime I (uniform structure, coherent closure). The temporary nature of the broadening — appearing specifically at the structural transition and disappearing once the transition is complete — confirms that the governing variable is geometric uniformity, not pressure magnitude.

**YBCO under pressure.** External pressure applied to YBa$_2$Cu$_3$O$_{7-\delta}$ does not raise $T_c$ appreciably — the small yttrium ion already exerts significant "chemical pressure" — but causes a broadening of the resistive transition. ISE interprets this as the system being already near the boundary between Regime I and Regime II: the existing internal pressure places the lattice close to the structural tolerance limit for uniform dimensional reduction within the CuO$_2$ planes, and additional external pressure pushes it into the percolative regime.

## Gradual Superconductivity as a Distinct Phase

The standard theoretical framework treats superconductivity as a binary phenomenon: the gap equation is either satisfied or it is not, and the transition is a thermodynamic phase transition (second order in BCS theory). The experimentally observed broadened and incomplete transitions under pressure are therefore treated as anomalies, attributed to sample inhomogeneity, non-hydrostatic pressure conditions, or coexisting structural phases.

ISE reframes this situation. The broadened transition is not an anomaly but the natural manifestation of a distinct physical regime — *stochastic resonance closure* — that lies between full coherent superconductivity and the normal state. In this regime:

1. The dimensional reduction that enforces closure operates locally but not globally. Individual domains achieve geometrically enforced complementarity, but the domains are not mutually phase-coherent.
2. The macroscopic order parameter $\Psi$ is not spatially uniform but fluctuates in amplitude and phase across the sample, reflecting the stochastic geometry of the closure domains.
3. Transport properties interpolate continuously between the superconducting and normal states, governed by the percolation statistics of the closure domain network.

This is structurally analogous to the relationship between a fully ordered ferromagnet, a partially ordered state with magnetic domains, and a paramagnet — but realized in the Cooper-pair channel rather than the spin channel. The analogy is not accidental: both phenomena involve the breakdown of macroscopic phase coherence into local, fluctuating coherence islands whose collective behavior is governed by connectivity rather than by the properties of any single domain.

The key distinction from standard theory is that ISE does not require an ad hoc percolation model superimposed on BCS. The stochastic closure regime emerges directly from the two-pathway framework: it is what happens when the conditions for Pathway II (dimensional reduction) are met locally but not globally. No additional mechanisms or models are needed. The same resonance closure condition that produces sharp transitions under uniform compression produces broadened transitions under non-uniform compression — the physics is identical; only the geometry differs.

This constitutes a falsifiable prediction: the transition from Regime I to Regime II should correlate with independently measurable structural indicators — specifically, the onset of lattice disorder, phase coexistence, domain formation, or structural incommensuration at the relevant pressure. The prediction is not that every pressure increase broadens the transition, but that broadening occurs specifically when the geometric uniformity of the dimensional reduction breaks down. Conversely, if a structural transition completes and a new uniform phase is established, the transition should sharpen again — as is observed in elemental sulfur above 155 GPa. The existing experimental evidence reviewed above is consistent with this prediction across multiple material families.
