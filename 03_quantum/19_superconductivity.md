## **3.19. Superconductivity as Macroscopic Resonance Closure**


The dominant theoretical framework for superconductivity is the BCS theory (Bardeen, Cooper, Schrieffer, 1957). In this account, an electron traversing a metallic lattice locally deforms the ionic structure through phonon emission. The resulting transient accumulation of positive charge density attracts a second electron. This phonon-mediated attraction overcomes the Coulomb repulsion between the two electrons, binding them into a Cooper pair — a composite boson with total spin zero and opposite momenta. Because Cooper pairs are bosons, they are exempt from the Pauli exclusion principle and may collectively occupy the same quantum ground state, forming a macroscopic Bose–Einstein condensate in momentum space. This condensate is described by a single wavefunction with a globally defined phase. Resistance vanishes because individual scattering events cannot eject a Cooper pair from the condensate: doing so would require overcoming the BCS energy gap $\Delta$, which thermal fluctuations below the critical temperature $T_c$ cannot provide. As long as no scattering process delivers sufficient energy to break a pair, no dissipative channel exists.

The BCS wavefunction is not a collection of identifiable pairs but a collective state over the entire Fermi sea:

$$|\Psi_{\text{BCS}}\rangle = \prod_{\mathbf{k}} \left( u_{\mathbf{k}} + v_{\mathbf{k}} \, c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow} c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow} \right) |0\rangle$$

For each momentum $\mathbf{k}$, the amplitudes $u_{\mathbf{k}}$ and $v_{\mathbf{k}}$ describe the probability of the state $(\mathbf{k}\!\uparrow, -\mathbf{k}\!\downarrow)$ being occupied or empty. The product runs over all $\mathbf{k}$ simultaneously. No individual electron is assigned to a specific partner; the pairing is a collective correlation structure across the entire momentum space. The coherence length $\xi$ — which can reach thousands of ångströms in conventional superconductors — measures the spatial range of this correlation, within which millions of such correlations overlap.

**Eliashberg Theory: Extension, Not Alternative**

Eliashberg theory (1960) is routinely presented as the strong-coupling generalization of BCS. The distinction is purely technical, not conceptual. BCS assumes weak electron-phonon coupling ($\lambda \ll 1$) and treats the phonon-mediated interaction as instantaneous, yielding a frequency-independent gap equation with a single energy scale $\Delta$. Eliashberg retains the frequency dependence: the gap function $\Delta(\omega)$ and renormalization function $Z(\omega)$ depend on energy because the phonons have finite frequency and the coupling is retarded. This is mathematically more involved but ontologically identical: electrons pair through phonon exchange, form a condensate, and the order parameter has a global phase.

What Eliashberg delivers over BCS are corrections to the universal BCS ratios. BCS predicts $2\Delta(0)/k_BT_c = 3.53$ for all superconductors. Lead yields 4.3 — BCS fails, Eliashberg corrects this accurately. But this is not new physics; it is a better approximation of the same physics. BCS is the zeroth order, Eliashberg the first order of a perturbation series in $\lambda$. Both share the same ontology (phonons as mediator, Cooper pairs as ground state), the same phase diagram topology (sharp transition at $T_c$), and the same blindness to everything beyond electron-phonon coupling.

The critical temperature is estimated through the McMillan-Allen-Dynes formula:

$$T_c = \frac{\omega_{\log}}{1.2} \exp\left[-\frac{1.04(1+\lambda)}{\lambda - \mu^*(1+0.62\lambda)}\right]$$

where $\omega_{\log}$ is the logarithmically averaged phonon frequency, $\lambda$ the electron-phonon coupling constant, and $\mu^{\ast}$ the Coulomb pseudopotential — a phenomenological parameter that absorbs the effect of screened electron-electron repulsion. The value of $\mu^*$ is not derived from the theory but assigned, typically between 0.10 and 0.15. This single free parameter carries substantial weight: small changes in $\mu^{\ast}$ shift $T_c$ predictions by tens of Kelvin.

**The Predictive Record**

The standard narrative presents the hydrogen-rich superhydrides as a triumph of Eliashberg theory: in 2014, Duan et al. predicted that $\text{H}_3\text{S}$ should exhibit $T_c$ of 191–204 K at ~200 GPa, and in 2015 Drozdov et al. measured $T_c \approx 203$ K at 155 GPa. The temperature value appears to match. However, a closer examination reveals significant complications.

The predicted pressure (~200 GPa) deviates from the experimental pressure (155 GPa) by nearly 25%. Since $T_c$ rises with pressure in these systems, a model that is correct at 200 GPa should predict a substantially lower $T_c$ at 155 GPa. The coincidence of the temperature value at the wrong pressure is not a clean prediction — it indicates either an overestimation of $T_c$ at the predicted pressure, a misprediction of the phase transition pressure, or a fortuitous cancellation of errors. Different theoretical groups arrived at conflicting predictions for the phase transition pressure and crystal symmetry.

Furthermore, the raw Eliashberg calculation in the harmonic approximation does not reproduce the experimental $T_c$. Agreement was achieved only after successive corrections:

1. **Anharmonic phonon corrections**: hydrogen atoms under megabar pressure deviate strongly from harmonic behavior. Including anharmonicity renormalizes the phonon frequencies and substantially modifies $\lambda$.
2. **Vertex corrections**: the adiabatic (Migdal) approximation breaks down in $\text{H}_3\text{S}$ because the phonon energy scale approaches the electronic energy scale. Lowest-order vertex corrections to the electron-phonon coupling are substantial.
3. **Full-bandwidth treatment**: the standard constant-density-of-states approximation fails for materials with pronounced features near the Fermi level. Retaining the full electronic structure changes $T_c$ by tens of Kelvin.

Only when all three corrections are applied simultaneously does $T_c$ fall within the experimental range. Each correction is physically motivated, but each also narrows the gap between prediction and known experimental result. The process is better characterized as convergent retroaction — iteratively refining the calculation with knowledge of the target — than as genuine prediction.

For $\text{LaH}_{10}$, the situation is similar: different groups predicted $T_c$ values ranging from 200 K to 290 K depending on methodology and parameter choices. With an experimental value of ~250 K, essentially every calculation can claim partial agreement.

The Allen-Dynes formula itself carries a systematic bias: it was originally calibrated on low $T_c$ superconductors and systematically underpredicts $T_c$ for high $T_c$ hydrides. This has been corrected through machine-learning-derived modifications to the formula — a procedure that replaces a physics-based prediction with a data-fitted correction.

The most reliable use of Eliashberg theory is in the conventional, weakly coupled regime ( $\lambda < 1$ ). A recent AI-accelerated pipeline screened 1.3 million candidate structures and identified 741 compounds with DFT-confirmed $T_c > 5$ K, of which two were synthesized and confirmed. This represents genuine predictive power — but in a regime where BCS alone would suffice.

Quantum Monte Carlo benchmarks have established that the Migdal-Eliashberg theory breaks down at renormalized coupling $\lambda \gtrsim 1.7$ — precisely the regime relevant for high-temperature superconductivity. The breakdown is not associated with an instability toward any ordered ground state but with the local physics of classical bipolaron formation. In the regime where predictive power is most needed, the theory is formally invalid.

**The Broader Theoretical Landscape**

No alternative framework has resolved the situation. The field is characterized by a proliferation of class-specific models, each applicable to a narrow material family, with no unifying mechanism across classes.

**Superconducting Density Functional Theory (SCDFT)** replaces the Eliashberg self-energy with a density-functional approach. It eliminates $\mu^{\ast}$ as a free parameter by computing the Coulomb interaction from first principles. However, SCDFT systematically underestimates $T_c$, with dramatic errors for several important materials. Recent improvements to the exchange-correlation functionals have narrowed the gap, but at high coupling strength the functional approximations exceed their validity range, and Eliashberg is considered more reliable. SCDFT is a parallel computational path, not a conceptual advance.

**Spin fluctuation theories** replace phonons with antiferromagnetic fluctuations as the pairing mediator, retaining the Eliashberg mathematical structure. For bulk FeSe, an Eliashberg treatment with embedded spin fluctuations yields $T_c \approx 6$ K, consistent with the experimental value of ~8 K. For monolayer FeSe on $\text{SrTiO}_3$ — where $T_c$ exceeds 100 K — the same framework fails entirely. The theory succeeds for one system and fails for its close relative, revealing that the choice of mediator does not constitute a predictive principle.

**Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DMFT) and its extensions** provide sophisticated numerical tools for computing electronic correlations, phase diagrams, and spectral properties. They have been applied to cuprate and nickelate superconductors with some success in reproducing qualitative features. However, these are computational methods for evaluating given Hamiltonians, not theories of superconductivity. They do not predict which materials will superconduct or at what temperature without substantial input from experiment.

**Anderson's Resonating Valence Bond (RVB) theory** (1987) proposed that cuprate superconductivity arises from a spin-liquid state in a doped Mott insulator. RVB correctly anticipated d-wave pairing symmetry — a genuine qualitative prediction. However, the framework has not produced quantitative $T_c$ predictions and remains a qualitative phenomenological model rather than a calculational tool.

**Holographic superconductivity** establishes a mathematical duality between quantum-critical Eliashberg theory and gravitational models in Anti-de Sitter space. It provides formal insight into the structure of pairing near quantum critical points but operates at a level of abstraction that yields no material-specific predictions.

The situation has been characterized within the community as "untenable": each material class appears to require its own class-specific mechanism. Cuprates may pair through spin fluctuations, iron-based superconductors through orbital fluctuations or spin-orbital interplay, heavy-fermion systems through magnetic interactions, hydrides through conventional phonons under extreme conditions, and nickelates through a mechanism not yet identified. No framework provides a unified account. The formal definition of "unconventional superconductor" — a material whose superconductivity is not explained by BCS or Eliashberg theory — encompasses the majority of materials with $T_c$ above 30 K.

**Spectral Reformulation**

Within the ISE framework, superconductivity is the establishment of macroscopic phase coherence in the electromagnetic frequency band. The phenomenon requires no new ontological category; it is a direct consequence of the same resonance closure conditions that govern particle stability, confinement, and binding throughout the theory. The framework identifies two distinct pathways to closure — *dynamic stabilization* and *dimensional reduction* — which correspond to qualitatively different physical regimes but share the same underlying mechanism.

The unified treatment addresses the central deficit of the standard landscape: the proliferation of class-specific mechanisms. ISE does not require a choice between phonons, magnons, charge fluctuations, or orbital interactions as competing pairing mediators. These are different projective descriptions of the same underlying process — complementary spectral deficits closing into a coherent resonance structure. The dominant frequency-band structure of the lattice determines which projective description appears most natural in each material class, but the mechanism is always resonance closure.

**Frequency Profiles and Spectral Deficits.** Every conduction electron is a frequency profile — an amplitude-phase distribution across hierarchically sampled differentiation bands. In a normal metal, these profiles possess spectral deficits: frequency bands in which the amplitude-phase structure is not self-consistent. These open bands are the spectral signature of what conventional theory describes as available states near the Fermi surface.

**Complementary Deficits and Pair Formation.** When two frequency profiles possess complementary spectral deficits — open bands whose amplitude-phase structures are mutually completing — their superposition generates a composite pattern at a higher differentiation depth. The previously open bands are superseded by the emergent spectral structure of the superposition. This is the ISE description of Cooper pair formation: two profiles whose deficits are complementary (in BCS language: time-reversed states $(\mathbf{k}\!\uparrow, -\mathbf{k}\!\downarrow)$ ) combine into a closed resonance structure that neither profile contained individually.

**Resonance Closure as Self-Consistency.** The composite profile satisfies a closure condition: its internal phase relations are self-resonant and require no external stabilization. This is formally identical to the non-trivial solution of the BCS gap equation,

$$\Delta_{\mathbf{k}} = -\sum_{\mathbf{k}'} V_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}'} \frac{\Delta_{\mathbf{k}'}}{2E_{\mathbf{k}'}},$$

which admits either the trivial solution $\Delta = 0$ (normal state: no resonance closure) or a non-trivial solution $\Delta \neq 0$ (superconducting state: closure achieved). The ISE term *resonance closure* designates precisely this bifurcation: the self-consistency condition is satisfied, and the frequency profile space admits a stable, closed configuration.

**The Energy Gap as Closure Stability.** The BCS gap $\Delta$ corresponds to the minimum energy required to disrupt the resonance closure locally — to re-open spectral bands that the composite profile had closed. Below $T_c$, the thermal broadband frequency structure of the lattice lacks sufficient spectral overlap with the closed profile to induce phase drift. Above $T_c$, the thermal spectrum overlaps sufficiently with the paired profile's frequency content to destroy the phase coherence that sustains closure.

**Macroscopic Phase Coherence.** In the superconducting state, the resonance closure extends across the entire sample. The macroscopic order parameter $\Psi = |\Psi|e^{i\varphi}$ with a globally defined phase $\varphi$ is the direct expression of this: the closed profile structure is not local but system-wide. This is the analogue in the electromagnetic frequency band of the color-neutral closure condition that defines hadrons in the strong-interaction band — but realized at macroscopic coherence length rather than at femtometer scales.

**Why Eliashberg Works When It Works.** The Eliashberg framework succeeds for conventional superconductors because density functional theory accurately computes the electronic band structure — and the band structure *is* the projective representation of the phase-space geometry. Every correction that improves Eliashberg predictions (anharmonicity, vertex corrections, full bandwidth) amounts to a more accurate description of this geometry. The convergence toward experiment reflects not the correctness of the phonon-mediation ontology but the increasing fidelity of the geometric description. ISE predicts that any sufficiently accurate characterization of the phase-space geometry will yield correct $T_c$ values — regardless of whether the theoretical language uses phonons, spin fluctuations, or resonance closure. The failures of Eliashberg in the unconventional regime occur precisely where the phase-space geometry cannot be captured by a single-boson-exchange perturbation expansion — where the closure involves multiple frequency bands simultaneously and the projective decomposition into a single mediator breaks down.

**Two Pathways to Closure**

The resonance closure condition can be satisfied through two fundamentally different mechanisms. Their distinction is central to understanding why superconductivity appears across such diverse material classes with such different critical temperatures.

**Pathway I: Dynamic Stabilization.** In conventional superconductors at ambient or moderate pressures, the electronic phase space is high-dimensional. Electrons possess many degrees of freedom into which they can scatter, and the Fermi surface is a complex manifold in momentum space. Resonance closure in this regime is a *dynamic* achievement: the lattice (via phonons, spin fluctuations, or other mediators) provides an effective attraction that correlates complementary deficit profiles across the Fermi surface. The closure is maintained against thermal disruption through continuous dynamic stabilization — a delicate balance between the attractive correlation and the entropic tendency toward decoherence.

In this regime, the coherence length $\xi$ is a meaningful and central parameter. It measures the spatial range over which the dynamic correlation can be sustained. Large $\xi$ (thousands of ångströms in elemental superconductors) reflects weak coupling: the correlation is fragile and extends over many lattice constants precisely because it must recruit a large volume of phase space to achieve closure. The BCS gap $\Delta$ is correspondingly small, and $T_c$ is low.

**Pathway II: Dimensional Reduction.** Under extreme compression, the available electronic phase space collapses. The lattice geometry constrains the electronic degrees of freedom so severely that the frequency profiles of the conduction electrons are forced into a low-dimensional subspace. In the limiting case, the profiles are confined to a single axis with only two possible phase orientations — complementary by construction.

In this regime, resonance closure is not dynamically stabilized but *geometrically enforced*. Scattering is eliminated not because an energy gap protects the paired state, but because the phase space contains no states into which scattering could occur. The closure is trivial in the mathematical sense: the self-consistency condition is automatically satisfied when the space of solutions contains only complementary configurations.

The coherence length $\xi$ loses its physical significance in this regime. The concept presupposes that decoherence is possible and that the correlation has a finite range — but when the phase space itself admits only closed configurations, coherence is not a property that can degrade with distance. It is a structural identity of the available state space. Asking for the coherence length is like asking how far a one-lane road can maintain single-file traffic: the question presupposes alternatives that do not exist.

This distinction reframes the anomalously high $T_c$ values of the superhydrides. The standard explanation within Eliashberg theory appeals to exceptionally strong electron-phonon coupling ($\lambda \approx 2.0$) combined with high phonon frequencies — both consequences of the light hydrogen mass under megabar compression. But this explanation encounters the very breakdown of the Migdal-Eliashberg framework at $\lambda \gtrsim 1.7$ that quantum Monte Carlo benchmarks have established. The theory invokes coupling strengths that exceed its own validity range.

ISE resolves this tension: the extreme pressure does not merely strengthen the coupling within a high-dimensional phase space; it *collapses the phase space itself*. The light hydrogen atoms under compression have both extremely high characteristic frequencies and extremely restricted geometric freedom. The result is not stronger dynamic stabilization but a crossover toward dimensional reduction — closure becomes increasingly geometric rather than dynamic, and $T_c$ rises not because the coupling constant increases but because the conditions under which closure fails become progressively harder to realize. The large $\lambda$ values computed within Eliashberg theory are not measures of coupling strength in the dynamical sense; they are projections of the degree of phase-space collapse onto a formalism designed for the dynamical regime.

**High-Temperature Superconductors and Structural Predictions**

The ISE framework generates several structural predictions that align with the empirical properties of high-temperature superconductors. The two-pathway structure developed above provides the interpretive key.

**Layered Structure as Partial Dimensional Reduction.** All known high $T_c$ superconductor families at ambient pressure share a common structural feature: highly ordered, quasi-two-dimensional layers ( $\text{CuO}_2$ planes in cuprates, FeAs/FeSe layers in iron-based superconductors). ISE predicts this directly: the layered geometry represents a partial dimensional reduction at ambient pressure. The electronic degrees of freedom are constrained to quasi-two-dimensional manifolds, reducing the phase space available for scattering and lowering the threshold for closure. Disordered or three-dimensionally isotropic structures lack this geometric constraint and therefore require either lower temperatures (more dynamic stabilization) or external pressure (more dimensional reduction) to achieve closure. High $T_c$ materials at ambient pressure are layered because the crystal geometry performs a structural function that extreme pressure achieves by brute force.

**Anisotropy as Closure Directionality.** Cuprate superconductors are extremely anisotropic — their superconducting properties depend strongly on crystallographic direction, with coherence lengths of 1–10 Å perpendicular to the planes versus substantially longer values within the planes. ISE interprets this as the natural consequence of resonance closure establishing itself preferentially along the directions in which the dimensional reduction is most effective. Within the planes, the constrained phase space supports robust closure. Perpendicular to the planes, the full three-dimensional phase space is available, scattering channels proliferate, and coherence is correspondingly limited.

**Short Coherence Length at High $T_c$ .** The coherence length $\xi$ in high $T_c$ materials (1–10 Å) is orders of magnitude shorter than in conventional superconductors (thousands of Å). Within the two-pathway framework, this has a precise interpretation: high $T_c$ materials at ambient pressure occupy an intermediate position between pure dynamic stabilization (Pathway I, large $\xi$, low $T_c$) and pure dimensional reduction (Pathway II, $\xi$ undefined, high $T_c$). The short coherence length reflects the fact that the partial dimensional reduction in layered structures tightens the coupling — the closure is sustained over shorter distances because the reduced phase space makes it more robust. In the limit of full dimensional reduction under extreme pressure, $\xi$ ceases to be a relevant parameter entirely.

**Pressure Enhancement of $T_c$ as Phase-Space Collapse.** Hydrostatic pressure systematically raises $T_c$ across many superconducting families. The most dramatic examples are the superhydrides: $\mathrm{H_3S}$ achieves $T_c = 203$ K at 150 GPa, $\text{LaH}_{10}$ reaches $T_c \approx 250$ K at comparable pressures. ISE reads this not as stronger coupling within an unchanged phase space, but as progressive dimensional reduction of the phase space itself. Compression restricts the geometric degrees of freedom available to the conduction electrons, driving the system from Pathway I toward Pathway II. The crossover is continuous: as pressure increases, the closure becomes increasingly geometric and decreasingly dynamic, $T_c$ rises, and the coherence length loses interpretive significance. The superhydrides represent the most advanced point on this continuum currently accessible — systems in which the light hydrogen atoms under megabar compression approach the limit of geometrically enforced closure.

**Magnetism as Reorganizable Coherence.** Undoped cuprates are antiferromagnetic insulators. Doping destroys the magnetic order and, within a specific doping range, enables superconductivity. The coexistence of magnetic fluctuations near $T_c$ has led to proposals that spin fluctuations provide the pairing glue in cuprates. ISE offers a unified reading: magnetic order is itself a form of phase coherence — in the spin-frequency band rather than the charge-frequency band. Doping disrupts this rigid spin coherence, liberating spectral degrees of freedom whose deficit structure becomes available for reorganization into electromagnetic-band closure. The magnetic fluctuations observed near $T_c$ are not the pairing mechanism; they are the spectral residue of the dissolving spin coherence whose structural deficits now participate in charge-band resonance closure.

This reinterpretation dissolves the long-standing debate over the pairing mechanism in unconventional superconductors. The proliferation of class-specific mechanisms — phonons for elemental superconductors, spin fluctuations for cuprates, orbital fluctuations for iron-based systems, an unidentified mechanism for nickelates — is an artifact of projecting a single underlying process onto different representational bases. Resonance closure is mediator-agnostic: it requires only that the phase-space geometry admits complementary deficit structures. The lattice, the magnetic correlations, and the orbital structure are different aspects of the same phase-space geometry, and whichever aspect dominates the projective description varies by material class without altering the mechanism.

**Resonance Closure as a Valence Band Property**

The preceding analysis implies a further structural consequence that shifts the conceptual locus of superconductivity from the electron to the band.

**The Standard Locus: Electrons**

In BCS and Eliashberg theory, the superconducting instability is formulated in terms of electron pairs. The gap equation operates on quasiparticle states; the order parameter describes a condensate of paired electrons; the energy gap protects individual pairs against thermal disruption. The band structure enters as a fixed background — a stage on which the pairing drama unfolds, but not itself the actor.

**The Band Itself**

In the resonance closure framework, the self-consistency condition operates on the frequency profile space of the entire valence band. The closure is not a property of specific electron pairs but of the band's spectral deficit structure as a whole. Whether the deficits close depends on the global geometry of the band — its topology, its density of states profile, its symmetry constraints, and the distribution of spectral weight near the Fermi level. Individual electrons are participants in a collective configuration, not agents of it.

This distinction is not merely semantic. It generates a specific prediction: **the same electrons, introduced into the same material via different doping routes, may or may not produce superconductivity — depending on how the doping reshapes the band structure.**

**Experimental Confirmation: Doping Route Determines Closure**

This prediction is directly confirmed in strontium titanate ( $\text{SrTiO}_3$ ), a material that exhibits superconductivity at remarkably low carrier densities. When mobile electrons are introduced by removing oxygen atoms, the superconducting transition persists down to carrier densities of $2 \times 10^{17}$ cm $^{-3}$ . When the same carrier type is introduced by substituting titanium with niobium, the threshold density for superconductivity is an order of magnitude higher. The rigid band approximation — the assumption that doping merely shifts the Fermi level within a fixed band structure — fails completely. Oxygen vacancies sculpt a specific band that constitutes the exclusive locus of the superconducting instability in the ultradilute limit. Niobium substitution introduces the same carrier type without sculpting this band, and the closure threshold rises correspondingly.

The ISE reading is direct: the two doping routes produce different frequency profile geometries in the valence band. Oxygen vacancies create a band configuration in which spectral deficits are already nearly complementary at very low filling — the closure threshold is low because the band geometry is already close to self-consistent. Niobium substitution creates a different band configuration with less favorable deficit structure, requiring an order of magnitude more carriers to reach a comparable closure geometry.

**Doping as Band Sculpting**

This principle extends far beyond $\text{SrTiO}_3$:

**Semiconductors to superconductors.** Undoped diamond, silicon, germanium, and silicon carbide are wide-bandgap semiconductors with no metallic character. By heavy doping with boron or aluminum — introducing acceptors with specific ionization energies — these materials can be driven through an insulator-metal transition and into a superconducting state. The critical variable is not the carrier concentration alone but the ionization energy of the dopant, which determines how the impurity band merges with the valence band. In boron-doped SiC and aluminum-doped SiC, the same host lattice yields different superconductor types (Type I vs. Type II) depending on the acceptor's ionization energy. The dopant does not merely add carriers; it restructures the band's spectral profile.

**Topological insulators.** In SnTe, indium doping induces superconductivity. The standard expectation — that indium acts as a hole dopant — fails: the superconductivity emerges in a regime where electron carriers dominate. The doping does not increase the density of states at the Fermi level in the expected way; it alters the band topology. The closure condition is met not because more states are available for pairing, but because the band's deficit structure has been rearranged into a complementary configuration.

**Optimal doping windows.** First-principles calculations on elemental superconductors (Mo, Nb, Pb, Al, Zr) demonstrate that both electron and hole doping can increase $T_c$ through phonon softening and enhanced contributions from specific phonon modes. For each material, there exists an optimal doping level at which $T_c$ is maximized. Beyond this optimum, further doping degrades superconductivity. The ISE interpretation: the optimal doping level corresponds to the band configuration in which the spectral deficit complementarity is maximized. Below the optimum, the band is not yet in its most favorable closure geometry. Above it, the excess doping distorts the band past the optimal configuration, reopening deficits that had been closed.

**Cuprate doping diagrams.** The superconducting dome in cuprate phase diagrams — the characteristic rise and fall of $T_c$ as a function of hole doping — is the most prominent example. Underdoped cuprates have insufficient disruption of the antiferromagnetic spin coherence (too little spectral reorganization from spin band to charge band). Overdoped cuprates have too much — the excess doping disorders the band past the optimal deficit geometry. The dome is the resonance closure window: the range of doping levels within which the valence band's spectral deficit structure admits macroscopic closure.

**Implications: Superconductivity as a General Band Property**

If resonance closure is a property of the band geometry rather than a property of specific electron-mediator-electron interactions, then the question "which materials are superconductors?" is ill-posed. The correct question is: **"which doping configuration brings this material's valence band into a closure-capable geometry?"**

This reframing has a radical consequence: superconductivity is not a rare phenomenon confined to special material classes. It is a latent property of any electronic band structure that can, in principle, be activated by appropriate band sculpting. The experimental evidence — semiconductors turned superconducting by heavy doping, topological insulators turned superconducting by intercalation, normal metals with $T_c$ enhanced by targeted doping — is consistent with this view. The diversity of known superconductor families does not reflect a diversity of mechanisms; it reflects the diversity of routes by which different host band structures can be brought to spectral deficit complementarity.

The practical implication is that material design for superconductivity should target the band geometry directly rather than searching for materials with intrinsically strong electron-phonon coupling. The relevant design parameters are not coupling constants but spectral deficit distributions: the shape, symmetry, and topology of the density of states near the Fermi level, and the degree to which these can be driven toward complementary closure by chemical or structural modification. This is a fundamentally different optimization target from the one pursued within the Eliashberg framework — and one that is, in principle, accessible to modern computational band structure methods without the approximation chain that limits Eliashberg predictions.

**Toward Predictive Band Sculpting: Parameter Candidates**

The claim that superconductivity can be induced by targeted doping is only as strong as the ability to identify, computationally, which doping configurations will bring a given band structure to closure. The necessary computational infrastructure already exists — density functional theory routinely produces the full electronic band structure, density of states, and Fermi surface topology of arbitrary materials, and large databases (SuperBand, JARVIS-DFT, Materials Project, OQMD) catalogue these properties for thousands of compounds. What is missing is not data but the correct screening criterion.

**The Current Approach: Scalar Screening.** Existing high-throughput workflows for superconductor discovery screen candidates primarily by two scalar quantities: the electronic density of states at the Fermi level, $N(E_F)$, and the Debye temperature $\theta_D$ (or, when computationally feasible, the electron-phonon coupling constant $\lambda$ derived from DFT perturbation theory). These parameters are motivated by the BCS equation, which relates $T_c$ to $N(E_F)$ and the pairing interaction strength. Pipelines based on this approach have successfully identified conventional superconductors with $T_c > 5$ K from databases of over a thousand materials.

The limitation is fundamental: $N(E_F)$ is a scalar that counts available states but encodes nothing about their geometric arrangement. The $\text{SrTiO}_3$ results demonstrate that two doping routes producing comparable carrier densities and similar $N(E_F)$ can differ by an order of magnitude in their superconducting threshold — because the relevant variable is not how many states are available but how their spectral deficits are distributed. Screening by $N(E_F)$ alone is analogous to predicting whether a jigsaw puzzle can be completed by counting the pieces without checking whether they fit together.

**The ISE Criterion: Vectorial Screening.** The resonance closure framework identifies a set of band-structural features that, taken together, constitute a vectorial closure criterion. All are computable from standard DFT output; none requires the electron-phonon coupling calculation that dominates the computational cost of current workflows.

**1. Spectral Deficit Complementarity (DOS Symmetry).** The density of states in symmetric energy windows above and below $E_F$ encodes the degree to which the occupied and unoccupied states near the Fermi level mirror each other. High mirror symmetry of $N(E_F + \varepsilon)$ and $N(E_F - \varepsilon)$ over a characteristic energy scale $\varepsilon_0$ indicates that the deficit structure is already close to complementary — electron-like and hole-like excitations at the same energy scale see matching phase-space volumes. This is the most direct computable proxy for deficit complementarity. The relevant quantity is not $N(E_F)$ alone but the functional form of $N(E)$ in a window around $E_F$ whose width is set by the characteristic phonon energy scale (or, in ISE terms, the dominant lattice frequency).

**2. Van Hove Proximity.** Van Hove singularities — points at which the density of states diverges due to saddle points or extrema in the band dispersion — represent maxima of spectral weight concentration. When a Van Hove singularity lies close to $E_F$, a small doping shift can bring it into resonance position, dramatically enhancing the deficit complementarity. The Van Hove distance $\delta E_{vH} = |E_{vH} - E_F|$ is a direct measure of how much doping is needed to reach the optimal closure geometry. Materials with $\delta E_{vH}$ within a chemically accessible doping range are prime candidates for doping-induced superconductivity. This principle is already exploited implicitly — nickelate thin films under strain show enhanced $T_c$ precisely because compression shifts a band at the M-point toward $E_F$, increasing the local density of states — but it is not used as a systematic screening parameter.

**3. Fermi Surface Nesting.** The degree to which segments of the Fermi surface can be mapped onto each other by a single wavevector $\mathbf{q}$ measures the phase-space volume available for complementary pairing at that wavevector. Strong nesting — large parallel segments of the Fermi surface separated by a well-defined $\mathbf{q}$ — is the momentum-space signature of spectral deficit complementarity. The nesting quality is encoded in the static Lindhard susceptibility $\chi_0(\mathbf{q})$: a sharp peak at a specific $\mathbf{q}$ indicates strong complementarity at that wavevector. Two-dimensional or quasi-two-dimensional Fermi surfaces (cylindrical rather than spherical) have inherently stronger nesting than three-dimensional ones — this is the Fermi surface expression of the partial dimensional reduction that characterizes layered high $T_c$ materials.

**4. Fermi Surface Dimensionality.** The effective dimensionality of the Fermi surface — whether it is a closed three-dimensional manifold, an open cylinder, or a set of quasi-one-dimensional sheets — directly reflects the degree of phase-space reduction available at ambient pressure. Lower-dimensional Fermi surfaces restrict the scattering phase space and enhance nesting, both of which favor closure. This parameter connects the band-sculpting perspective to the dimensional reduction pathway developed earlier: layered materials achieve partial dimensional reduction through crystal geometry, and this is reflected in the topology of their Fermi surface.

**5. Dopant Ionization Energy Relative to Band Gap.** For semiconductor hosts, the ionization energy $E_A$ (acceptor) or $E_D$ (donor) of the dopant determines whether the impurity states form an isolated level within the gap or merge with the valence or conduction band. Only when the impurity band merges with the host band can the combined system exhibit the continuous spectral deficit structure required for closure. Deep dopants ($E_A$ or $E_D$ large) create localized states that introduce disorder without enabling closure; shallow dopants create extended states that modify the band geometry constructively. The type of superconductivity (Type I vs. Type II) observed in doped semiconductors correlates with the ionization energy — a direct signature of how the dopant reshapes the deficit structure.

**6. Lifshitz Transition Proximity.** Lifshitz transitions — topological changes in the Fermi surface triggered by doping, pressure, or strain — represent points at which the deficit structure reconfigures qualitatively. Near a Lifshitz transition, the density of states develops a logarithmic singularity (in 2D) or a step (in 3D), and the Fermi surface topology changes from closed to open or vice versa. These transitions are natural candidates for closure onset: the reconfiguration of the deficit structure may bring it into a complementary arrangement that did not exist on either side of the transition individually. Doping to the vicinity of a Lifshitz transition is a targeted strategy for inducing closure.

**An Integrated Screening Protocol.** None of these parameters is individually sufficient. The closure criterion is inherently multidimensional — it depends on the joint geometry of the band structure, Fermi surface, and density of states. An ISE-informed screening workflow would combine them into a composite closure score:

1. Pre-screen by $N(E_F)$ (necessary but not sufficient — eliminates insulators and low-DOS metals).
2. Evaluate DOS symmetry in a window $\pm \varepsilon_0$ around $E_F$ (deficit complementarity proxy).
3. Compute Van Hove distances and Lifshitz transition proximities (doping targets for closure optimization).
4. Analyze Fermi surface nesting quality via $\chi_0(\mathbf{q})$ (momentum-space complementarity).
5. Assess Fermi surface dimensionality (ambient-pressure dimensional reduction).
6. For semiconductor hosts: evaluate dopant ionization energies against band gap (band merging criterion).

This protocol uses exclusively DFT-level data — no electron-phonon coupling calculations required, eliminating the computational bottleneck of current workflows. The screening is not for "strong coupling" but for "closure-favorable geometry," a qualitatively different target that is cheaper to compute and, if the ISE framework is correct, more predictive.

The protocol constitutes a falsifiable research program: if ISE's identification of resonance closure as the mechanism is correct, materials passing all six criteria at high scores should exhibit superconductivity upon appropriate doping, even if they are not flagged by conventional $N(E_F)$-and-$\lambda$ screening. Conversely, materials with high $N(E_F)$ but poor deficit complementarity should resist superconductivity despite favorable conventional indicators. The existing databases — containing thousands of computed band structures alongside experimental $T_c$ values or confirmed absence of superconductivity — provide the immediate testing ground.

**Closure Configuration Count and Supercurrent Path Geometry**

**From Six Parameters to One: The Configuration Count**

The six screening parameters introduced above are not independent variables. They are projections of a single underlying quantity: the **effective closure configuration count** — the number of independent pathways through which the self-consistency condition on the band geometry can be simultaneously satisfied.

Each band crossing the Fermi level represents a potential closure channel. But not every band contributes equally. A band contributes a closure configuration only if it possesses complementary deficit structure (adequate nesting), sufficient spectral weight concentration near $E_F$ (Van Hove proximity), and restricted scattering phase space (low dimensionality). The six parameters evaluate these conditions per band. The configuration count is their sum across all bands — weighted by quality.

This reduction to a single scalar generates a sharp, testable prediction: **$T_c$ should correlate with the effective configuration count, not with coupling strength.** High $T_c$ arises not because the pairing interaction is strong, but because the band geometry provides many independent closure pathways. Thermal fluctuations can destroy individual pathways, but the system remains superconducting as long as at least one pathway is closed. More pathways means higher temperatures can be tolerated before all are simultaneously disrupted.

### Empirical Correlation

The known superconductor families are ordered by this criterion with striking consistency:

**Conventional elemental superconductors** ($T_c < 10$ K): typically one band at $E_F$, simple Fermi surface, one closure pathway. Mercury, lead, niobium — single s-band systems with minimal configuration space.

**MgB$_2$** ($T_c = 39$ K): two distinct band groups at $E_F$ — the quasi-2D $\sigma$-bands and the 3D $\pi$-bands, with two separate gaps. Two independent closure channels. $T_c$ roughly quadruples relative to elemental superconductors.

**Iron-based superconductors** ($T_c$ up to 56 K, thin-film FeSe $> 100$ K): at least three of the five Fe 3d orbitals are active near $E_F$, forming multiple Fermi surface sheets with distinct orbital characters. Five bands crossing the Fermi level, complex nesting between electron and hole pockets. The configuration space is large, but the nesting quality and orbital overlap vary across families — which explains why $T_c$ varies dramatically within the iron-based class despite similar band counts.

**Cuprates** ($T_c$ up to 133 K at ambient pressure): superficially a single-band system ($d_{x^2-y^2}$), but with orbital hybridization ($d_{z^2}$ admixture), a Van Hove singularity at or near $E_F$ at optimal doping, and Fermi surface reconstruction through Lifshitz transitions under doping. The effective configuration count is not determined by band number alone but by the topological complexity of the Fermi surface — which reconfigures qualitatively across the doping diagram. At optimal doping, the Van Hove singularity sits at $E_F$ and the Fermi surface topology maximizes nesting. This has been noted independently: a detailed comparison of electronic band structures across cuprate and iron-based families concludes that the general mechanism of $T_c$ enhancement is likely related to the proximity of Van Hove singularities to the Fermi level — and that this is not related to the density of states but to "some kind of resonance caused by the proximity of the Fermi surface to a topological Lifshitz transition." The author uses the word "resonance" without having a framework for it.

**Hydrides under pressure** ($T_c$ up to 250 K): LaH$_{10}$ has one of the most complex Fermi surfaces among known superconductors — multiple nested cage-like sheets with numerous closure channels. The extreme pressure enforces dimensional reduction per channel (Pathway II), while the multiplicity of channels provides the configuration count. The combination — many pathways, each geometrically enforced — produces the highest known $T_c$ values.

The pattern is consistent: $T_c$ scales with the product of configuration count and per-channel closure quality, not with coupling strength. This is the single scalar that the six parameters collapse onto.

**Supercurrent Path Geometry**

The configuration count has a direct physical consequence that has not been recognized in the standard framework: **the supercurrent does not necessarily flow along the macroscopic conductor axis.**

If the closure pathway that is currently active — the band configuration through which the self-consistency condition is satisfied — has its nodal structure oriented at an angle to the crystal axis, then the Cooper-pair condensate propagates along that angle, not along the wire. The macroscopic current is the projection of the microscopic closure path onto the conductor geometry. In a material with a single closure pathway aligned with the crystal axis, the two coincide. In a material with multiple pathways at different orientations, the effective current path is the resultant of all active pathways — which may meander, zigzag, or spiral through the material.

This is not a theoretical extrapolation. It is directly observed:

**Nickelate superconductors.** In Nd$_{0.8}$Sr$_{0.2}$NiO$_2$, angle-resolved transport measurements reveal that the electrical conductivity peaks at 125° relative to the crystal principal axis in the normal state — but this angle shifts to 160° near the zero-resistance temperature. The superconducting phase stiffness maximizes along 160°, a direction that coincides with neither the electronic nematicity axis nor the crystal lattice symmetry. The supercurrent follows the closure geometry, not the crystal geometry, and the two are misaligned by a measurable angle.

**Bi-2212 round wires.** Bi$_2$Sr$_2$CaCu$_2$O$_x$ is the only high-$T_c$ superconductor that functions as a round wire with high critical current density — all others must be fabricated as tapes aligned along the crystal anisotropy. The mechanism enabling this is "c-axis brick-wall current flow": when in-plane transport is blocked by grain boundaries, the supercurrent switches to a zigzag path along the c-axis, hopping between misoriented grains through their basal-plane faces. The grain-to-grain misorientation (~15° FWHM) is far beyond the conventionally accepted strong-coupling range, yet the wire carries high current. The standard framework calls this a "conundrum." The ISE reading: the closure configuration count of Bi-2212 is high enough that at least one pathway remains closed even when the current must meander through misoriented grains.

**Intrinsic Josephson junctions in cuprates.** In highly anisotropic cuprates such as BSCCO, the supercurrent between CuO$_2$ planes flows via Josephson tunneling across the blocking layers. A single crystal naturally forms a stack of nanoscale Josephson junctions. The current does not traverse the crystal continuously but hops discretely from plane to plane — each hop adding path length and introducing a phase shift that depends on the interlayer coupling geometry.

**Superconducting diodes.** In vertical Josephson junctions with non-centrosymmetric barriers (Td-WTe$_2$), the supercurrent flows more easily in one direction than the other — a non-reciprocity directly coupled to the crystal structure of the barrier. The current path asymmetry reflects the asymmetry of the closure geometry in momentum space. The barrier's crystal structure imposes a preferred direction on the closure pathway, breaking the assumption that supercurrent is geometrically isotropic.

### Implications for Magnetic Field Generation

If the supercurrent path is not aligned with the macroscopic conductor geometry, this has direct consequences for magnetic field generation — the primary technological application of superconductors.

In a solenoid, the magnetic field is $B = \mu_0 n I$, calculated under the assumption that the current flows in circular loops perpendicular to the solenoid axis. If the actual current path deviates from this assumption — following the closure geometry rather than the conductor geometry — then:

1. **The effective field per ampere is material-dependent beyond $\lambda$ and $\xi$.** Two superconductors carrying the same current through the same coil geometry may produce different magnetic fields if their closure path geometries differ. The deviation is determined by the angle between the dominant closure pathway and the conductor axis.

2. **Materials with high configuration counts may produce weaker fields per cross-section.** If many closure pathways are active at different orientations, the net current direction is the vector sum of the individual pathway contributions. Pathways at large angles to the conductor axis contribute less to the macroscopic field while still carrying dissipationless current. The material is superconducting — zero resistance — but the effective current density contributing to field generation is reduced by the geometric factor.

3. **The effect is measurable as a discrepancy between calculated and observed field strength.** For a known current, conductor geometry, and winding pattern, the expected field is calculable from classical electrodynamics. Any systematic deviation — material-dependent, reproducible, and correlated with band structure anisotropy — would constitute direct evidence for closure path geometry influencing macroscopic electromagnetic properties.

This prediction connects the microscopic band geometry to a macroscopic, technologically relevant observable. It is falsifiable: if the closure path geometry has no influence on field generation, all superconductors with the same critical current density in the same coil geometry should produce identical fields. If the prediction is correct, materials with known anisotropic closure paths (cuprates, nickelates) should show systematic field deviations relative to isotropic superconductors (elemental metals) — and the magnitude of the deviation should correlate with the angle between the dominant closure pathway and the conductor axis as determined from band structure calculations.

**Stochastic Resonance Closure and the Limits of Dimensional Reduction**

The relationship between pressure and superconductivity is not monotonic. The two-pathway framework predicts a specific failure mode: dimensional reduction enhances closure only as long as the compression is globally uniform — only as long as all electronic profiles are forced into the same reduced subspace across the entire sample. When the lattice can no longer sustain this uniformity, the system enters a qualitatively distinct regime.

ISE predicts a sequence of three regimes as pressure increases:

**Regime I: Coherent Closure.** At moderate pressures, the lattice retains its ordered structure. Compression enhances closure — either by strengthening dynamic stabilization (moderate pressure on layered materials) or by progressing toward dimensional reduction (higher pressure, especially in hydrogen-rich systems). The superconducting transition is sharp: the gap equation has a clean non-trivial solution, the order parameter is spatially homogeneous, and resistance drops abruptly to zero.

**Regime II: Stochastic Percolative Closure.** At higher pressures, the lattice undergoes structural transitions — phase boundaries between crystal structures emerge, local symmetry breaking occurs, and the uniform geometry that supported global dimensional reduction fragments into domains. Within each domain, the local phase-space reduction may still enforce closure. But adjacent domains, separated by structural defects, phase boundaries, or symmetry mismatches, are not mutually coherent. The dimensional reduction succeeds locally but fails globally.

In this regime, the system consists of superconducting islands — local agglomerates in which resonance closure is geometrically enforced — embedded in a matrix of normal or weakly conducting material. Superconductivity in the macroscopic sense depends on percolation: a current can traverse the sample without dissipation if and only if a connected path of closed domains spans the sample. Because the closure domains fluctuate temporally — they form, persist, and dissolve as thermal and structural fluctuations modulate the local phase-space geometry — the percolative path is stochastic. At any given moment, a path is statistically likely but not guaranteed.

The observable consequences are:

- **Broadened transition**: $T_c$ is no longer a sharp bifurcation point but a gradual crossover. The resistance decreases continuously over a temperature range as the density and connectivity of closure domains increases with cooling.
- **Incomplete transition**: The resistance may not reach zero even at the lowest temperatures, because the percolative path is intermittent. Residual resistance persists — far below the normal-state value but measurably above zero.
- **Reduced critical current density**: The stochastic path can sustain less current than a homogeneous superconductor, because the effective cross-section of the connected superconducting path fluctuates and the weakest link determines the capacity.

**Regime III: Closure Collapse.** At still higher pressures, the structural deformation becomes so severe that even local dimensional reduction fails. No domain retains sufficient geometric order to enforce phase-space collapse. The electronic profiles return to a high-dimensional, disordered configuration in which neither dynamic stabilization (no coherent lattice to mediate coupling) nor dimensional reduction (no uniform compression axis) can produce closure. The material reverts to a normal or non-metallic state.

The critical structural variable governing the transitions between regimes is not pressure per se but *geometric uniformity of the dimensional reduction*. Pressure that preserves lattice order drives the system deeper into Regime I. Pressure that induces structural phase transitions, grain boundaries, or amorphization pushes the system into Regime II and eventually Regime III. This is why the regime boundaries are material-dependent and sensitive to pressure conditions (hydrostatic vs. non-hydrostatic), crystal quality, and proximity to structural phase transitions.

**Experimental Evidence for Stochastic Closure**

The predicted sequence of regimes is directly observed in several high-pressure superconductor systems:

**Mercury-based cuprates (Hg1212, Hg1223).** In $\mathrm{HgBa_2}$ $\mathrm{CaCu_2}$ $\mathrm{O_{6+\delta}}$, the superconducting transition broadens conspicuously under pressure in the overdoped regime. The same broadening appears in the underdoped regime at pressures above 15 GPa, precisely where $T_c$ begins to decrease. The authors of the systematic study describe the broadening as unexplained within their framework. ISE identifies it as the onset of Regime II: the lattice structure at these pressures can no longer sustain the geometric uniformity required for coherent closure across the sample.

**Iron-based superconductor $\mathrm{FeSe_{0.89}}$ $\mathrm{S_{0.11}}$ .** Under increasing pressure, this compound exhibits a sequence that maps precisely onto the three ISE regimes. Below 4 GPa, $T_c$ increases sharply (Regime I). Above 4 GPa, the transition broadens significantly; above 5 GPa, the resistance no longer reaches zero even at the lowest accessible temperatures, accompanied by an anomalously small critical current density (Regime II). The normal-state resistivity simultaneously develops non-metallic character. The authors describe this as "electronic phase separation" resulting from a structural transition — a descriptive label that ISE replaces with a mechanistic account: the structural transition has fragmented the globally uniform dimensional reduction into isolated domains that achieve closure only locally and stochastically.

**Elemental sulfur under extreme pressure.** Between 119 and 136 GPa, sulfur exhibits a broadened resistance drop during the gradual transition between crystal phases (body-centered orthorhombic S-IV to $\beta$-Po S-V). Above 155 GPa, the transition sharpens again as the sample fully enters the S-V phase. This sequence is a direct illustration of the regime structure: the phase coexistence region is Regime II (neither crystal structure spans the sample, closure is percolative across phase-mixed regions), while the homogeneous S-V phase restores Regime I (uniform structure, coherent closure). The temporary nature of the broadening — appearing specifically at the structural transition and disappearing once the transition is complete — confirms that the governing variable is geometric uniformity, not pressure magnitude.

**YBCO under pressure.** External pressure applied to $\mathrm{YBa_2}$ $\mathrm{Cu_3}$ $\mathrm{O_{7-\delta}}$ does not raise $T_c$ appreciably — the small yttrium ion already exerts significant "chemical pressure" — but causes a broadening of the resistive transition. ISE interprets this as the system being already near the boundary between Regime I and Regime II: the existing internal pressure places the lattice close to the structural tolerance limit for uniform dimensional reduction within the $\text{CuO}_2$ planes, and additional external pressure pushes it into the percolative regime.

**Gradual Superconductivity as a Distinct Phase**

The standard theoretical framework treats superconductivity as a binary phenomenon: the gap equation is either satisfied or it is not, and the transition is a thermodynamic phase transition (second order in BCS theory). The experimentally observed broadened and incomplete transitions under pressure are therefore treated as anomalies, attributed to sample inhomogeneity, non-hydrostatic pressure conditions, or coexisting structural phases.

ISE reframes this situation. The broadened transition is not an anomaly but the natural manifestation of a distinct physical regime — *stochastic resonance closure* — that lies between full coherent superconductivity and the normal state. In this regime:

1. The dimensional reduction that enforces closure operates locally but not globally. Individual domains achieve geometrically enforced complementarity, but the domains are not mutually phase-coherent.
2. The macroscopic order parameter $\Psi$ is not spatially uniform but fluctuates in amplitude and phase across the sample, reflecting the stochastic geometry of the closure domains.
3. Transport properties interpolate continuously between the superconducting and normal states, governed by the percolation statistics of the closure domain network.

This is structurally analogous to the relationship between a fully ordered ferromagnet, a partially ordered state with magnetic domains, and a paramagnet — but realized in the Cooper-pair channel rather than the spin channel. The analogy is not accidental: both phenomena involve the breakdown of macroscopic phase coherence into local, fluctuating coherence islands whose collective behavior is governed by connectivity rather than by the properties of any single domain.

The key distinction from standard theory is that ISE does not require an ad hoc percolation model superimposed on BCS. The stochastic closure regime emerges directly from the two-pathway framework: it is what happens when the conditions for Pathway II (dimensional reduction) are met locally but not globally. No additional mechanisms or models are needed. The same resonance closure condition that produces sharp transitions under uniform compression produces broadened transitions under non-uniform compression — the physics is identical; only the geometry differs.

This constitutes a falsifiable prediction: the transition from Regime I to Regime II should correlate with independently measurable structural indicators — specifically, the onset of lattice disorder, phase coexistence, domain formation, or structural incommensuration at the relevant pressure. The prediction is not that every pressure increase broadens the transition, but that broadening occurs specifically when the geometric uniformity of the dimensional reduction breaks down. Conversely, if a structural transition completes and a new uniform phase is established, the transition should sharpen again — as is observed in elemental sulfur above 155 GPa. The existing experimental evidence reviewed above is consistent with this prediction across multiple material families.
