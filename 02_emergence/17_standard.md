**Two Spectral Primitives**

The conventional Standard Model organizes physical reality through four fundamental forces — electromagnetism, the strong interaction, the weak interaction, and gravitation — each mediated by distinct carrier particles and governed by separate coupling constants. This quadripartite division, while operationally successful, conceals a deeper structural unity that the ISE framework exposes through ontological reduction.

The reduction proceeds from a single insight: all physical structure consists of relations within a differentiation field whose natural representation is spectral. These relations are of exactly two kinds.

**No Global Frequency: Differentiation Is Local**

The frequency-domain formalism as presented in the preceding chapters carries an implicit risk: the reader may assume that "frequency" refers to oscillation against a universal clock — a global time parameter against which all spectral content is defined. This assumption must be explicitly dissolved.

There is no global clock in ISE. There is no universal time parameter, and consequently there is no global definition of frequency. The sampling rates $R_k$ introduced are not measured against an external standard; they are *relational* — defined only with respect to other sampling rates within the same local differentiation structure. What counts as "high frequency" or "low frequency" is meaningful only relative to a locally established reference, not against an absolute spectral axis.

More precisely: not even within a single scale can a uniform frequency be coherently defined across all regions. Differentiation proceeds locally. Two regions of the differentiation field at the "same scale" need not — and generically will not — share a common frequency reference. Their spectral structures are locally self-consistent but mutually incommensurable until a superposition event establishes a joint reference.

This is the origin of the relative aspect of time. What we experience as temporal simultaneity is a locally negotiated phase relation, not a globally synchronized clock tick. Two spatially separated processes "happen at the same time" only to the extent that an observer's frequency profile can superimpose with both and establish a joint differentiation depth. The relativity of simultaneity in special and general relativity is, in this framework, not a geometric property of spacetime but the direct consequence of locally defined, non-globally-synchronized differentiation.

The practical consequence for the spectral formalism: when we write $\mathcal{P}(\omega)$ for a particle's frequency profile, the frequency axis $\omega$ is implicitly local. Comparing two profiles requires establishing a shared reference — which is itself a physical process (interaction, measurement, superposition). The photon, as the torsor origin in SRM, provides the minimal relational bridge for such comparison, but it does not supply a global frequency standard. It supplies a local one, propagated at $c$ — the maximum rate at which local frequency references can be relationally extended.


**Inter-Band Phase Structure — Parts to Each Other.** The first kind of relation describes how differentiated instances maintain phase coherence across frequency bands. As established spatial distance is not geometric separation but phase difference in the spatial meta-band:

$$d(\mathcal{P}_1, \mathcal{P}_2) \sim \text{phase difference in spatial meta-band}$$

$$E_{\text{pattern}} = \frac{A_{\text{pattern}}(f)}{\langle A_{\text{neighbors}}(f) \rangle}$$

This is the energetic relation — a local comparison of how much a pattern "stands out" within its frequency domain. What conventional physics labels "electromagnetism" is the most direct spectral projection of this principle, but the strong and weak interactions are equally expressions of it — symmetry-modified, band-shifted variants of the same intra-band amplitude relation.

**Stability as Projective Accumulation, Not Energetic Preference**

A persistent misreading must be preempted: frequency profiles do not possess a tendency, drive, or preference toward closure, lower energy states, or spectral completeness. The language of "falling into stability" or "preferring closed configurations" is a narrative artifact of describing projection outcomes in causal terms. What occurs is strictly additive differentiation — superposition upon superposition, generating ever finer spectral content. Within this process, low-frequency bands occupy a privileged \*accumulative\* position for a purely structural reason: they are contained as subharmonics in a greater number of higher-frequency structures. Every high-frequency pattern that achieves any degree of multi-rate coherence necessarily contains low-frequency harmonics as downsampled components (as established: $R_j = n \cdot R_i \implies \Phi_i = \text{Downsample}_n(\Phi_j)$). Low bands therefore receive amplitude contributions from a combinatorially larger set of superposition events.

Simultaneously, destructive interference operates asymmetrically across bands. Configurations whose spectral structure is incoherent at low frequencies — where accumulated harmonics from diverse sources cancel rather than reinforce — lose amplitude precisely in the bands where accumulation is strongest. Over successive differentiation steps, this produces a filtering effect: patterns that are coherent at low frequencies are *disproportionately reinforced*, while patterns that are incoherent at low frequencies are *disproportionately suppressed*.

This is the entire mechanism behind what physics calls stability, attraction, bound states, and force-mediated structure formation. It is not that systems "seek" lower energy — it is that in the time-projection of additive differentiation, low-band-coherent configurations accumulate amplitude faster than they lose it, while low-band-incoherent configurations lose amplitude faster than they gain it. The result, viewed in projection, appears as a landscape of stable and unstable configurations with apparently causal transitions between them. But the causality is projective, not ontological. The underlying process is blind superposition; the apparent directionality is an artifact of the asymmetric accumulation structure of low-frequency bands.

This principle extends beyond electromagnetic and gravitational attraction to all field effects that exhibit apparent energetic preference: chemical bonding, nuclear binding, phase transitions, spontaneous symmetry breaking, and thermodynamic equilibration are all projective traces of the same low-band accumulation asymmetry operating at different sampling rates.

**Convergence, Sequentiality, and the Weight of Pattern**

A clarification is necessary to prevent a subtle but consequential misreading of the differentiation process. When we say that two frequency profiles "superimpose" to produce a composite structure, this must not be read as a discrete event — as though A and B collide at a moment and produce C. A and B converge simultaneously across their entire spectral breadth toward C, and C possesses as many frequency components as one chooses to project — ontologically, infinitely many. The process has no edges, no before-and-after boundary, no discrete "click" at which the old profiles vanish and the new one appears.

However — and this is the origin of everything we experience as temporal, sequential, and causal — from the perspective of any given resonance, convergence does not appear simultaneous. A resonance is a finite structure with finite spectral content. It cannot resolve the full breadth of convergence at once in realtion to others. It samples the process at its own sampling rate, and at that rate, the convergence of A and B toward C propagates *relationally and sequentially*: first the lowest shared harmonics converge (they are contained in both profiles as subsamples), then successively higher spectral components, each requiring finer resolution to register. What is ontologically simultaneous becomes projectively serial.

This is the relative time impression. It is not that convergence "takes time" in an absolute sense — it is that any finite-resolution observer necessarily experiences convergence as a sequence, because its sampling rate imposes an ordering on what it can resolve and when. The "speed" at which convergence propagates through the observer's accessible bands is the speed of interaction — and its upper bound is $c$, the maximum rate at which phase relations can be relationally extended, as established by the photon's role as minimal-structure torsor origin.

And it is why the range and speed of force propagation depend on spectral complexity: the coherence of a propagating profile decays as it sequentially traverses the spatial meta-band, and denser profiles accumulate phase-coherence loss faster because they carry more bands through which decoherence can occur.

Mass, time, inertia, and interaction range are not four separate phenomena requiring four explanations. **Force Range as Scale Descent**

The hierarchy of interaction ranges in the Standard Model — gravitation and electromagnetism with apparently infinite range, the weak interaction confined to $\sim 10^{-18}$ m, the strong interaction confined to $\sim 10^{-15}$ m — receives a unified explanation within the spectral framework. **Unified Range Hierarchy.** No force has categorically infinite range. No force categorically vanishes at a boundary.
